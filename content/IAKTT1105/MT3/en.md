## Purpose of this independent study

In M5 and S5 we studied presentation programs and their operations — *which button to press*. This independent study is about *what and why*: how a presentation should convince an audience and lead it to a decision.

:::note
**After this independent study you will be able to:**

- determine a presentation's goal and audience;
- formulate the key message and build a logical story;
- apply the four design principles;
- choose a suitable chart for the data;
- critically assess an existing presentation against these principles.
:::

## A presentation is a communication tool

The goal of a presentation is not the slides but **the audience's decision or action**: an investor provides money, a manager approves a project, a committee accepts a thesis. So the work starts not by opening the program but with three questions:

| Question | Example (a business plan for a bank loan) |
|---|---|
| **Who is listening?** | the credit committee: finance people, short on time, attentive to risk |
| **What do they need to know?** | how much profit the project brings, how the debt is repaid, how risks are managed |
| **What should they do after the presentation?** | approve a 500 million soum loan |

## The key message and the pyramid principle

The **key message** is the one-sentence content of the presentation: "Expanding the bakery pays back in 18 months and makes it possible to repay the loan safely within 3 years". If the listener remembers only one thing, it should be this.

**The pyramid principle** (Barbara Minto): the conclusion first, then 3–4 arguments supporting it, and under each argument the figures and facts. This is the reverse of the academic order ("introduction → method → results → conclusion"): a busy manager gets the main idea in the very first minute.

```text
            Approve the loan: the project pays back in 18 months
           /                    |                      \
   There is demand        Costs are calculated       Risks are managed
 (queues, orders)         (TCO, unit cost, price)    (insurance, collateral, reserve)
```

## Story structure

**SCR** (Situation — Complication — Resolution):

1. **Situation** — a fact the audience agrees with: "The bakery sells 2,000 loaves a day".
2. **Complication** — a change or a risk: "In the morning the queue is 30 minutes and 20% of buyers leave".
3. **Resolution** — the proposal and its result: "A second oven — +40% capacity, pays back in 18 months".

Each slide should be one step of the story. If reading only the slide titles in sequence still gives a logical story, the structure is good.

## "Assertion + evidence" slides

A typical slide: the title "Sales" and 6 bullet points. More effective: the title is a **full assertion** sentence, and the slide body is the **evidence** proving it (a chart, a picture, a table).

| Weak title | Strong title |
|---|---|
| Sales dynamics | Sales doubled in 2 years but have reached the capacity limit |
| Costs | A second oven pays back in 18 months |
| Risks | The main risks are covered by insurance and collateral |

## The four design principles

| Principle | Meaning | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Contrast** | the important thing clearly stands out | the key figure is large and coloured, the rest grey |
| **Repetition** | similar elements look the same | all titles in one font and size — the slide master (M5) |
| **Alignment** | every element is aligned to some line | grids and guides, no "roughly" placement |
| **Proximity** | related elements are close, unrelated ones apart | a chart and its caption together |

## Cognitive load

A person can process only a limited amount of information at once. Practical rules from multimedia research (R. Mayer):

- **Remove the extras**: decorative images, 3D effects and unnecessary animations distract attention.
- **Do not read the text aloud**: a listener cannot read and listen at the same time. Key words and images on the slide, the explanation from the speaker.
- **Signal**: show what to look at now with an arrow, colour or zoom.
- **Segment**: reveal a complex diagram step by step.

## Colour and fonts

- 2–3 main colours: one neutral (text), one primary (brand), one accent (for the most important thing).
- High contrast between background and text; light colours look faded on a projector.
- Do not use a red–green pair as the only carrier of meaning — people with colour blindness cannot tell them apart; add symbols (▲▼).
- Fonts: one or two families; body text at least 24 pt, titles 32 pt or larger.

## Choosing a chart by purpose

| What do you want to show? | Chart |
|---|---|
| Change over time | line |
| Comparing categories | column, or horizontal bar (for long names) |
| Parts of a whole | 100% stacked column; pie — only for 2–4 parts |
| The relationship of two indicators | scatter (MT2) |
| The composition of a change from a starting to a final value | waterfall |
| One important figure | not a chart — a large number and a caption |

:::warning
**Common mistakes**

- **Writing everything on the slide.** A slide is not a document; detailed information goes into a handout.
- **A presentation without a key message.** The listener is left asking "So what should we do?"
- **A column chart whose axis does not start at zero.** A small difference looks huge and misleads the audience.
- **Too many effects.** A different animation on every slide is not professional.
:::

## Delivery

- **Time**: on average 1–2 minutes per slide; 6–8 slides for 10 minutes.
- **Rehearsal**: at least twice aloud, timing yourself.
- **Opening**: the key message in the first 30 seconds.
- **Questions**: prepare backup slides (an "appendix") for expected questions.
- **Close**: the key message and a clear request ("We ask you to approve a 500 million soum loan for 3 years").

## Assignment

1. Choose one economic presentation from the Internet or company reports (an investor report, a government programme presentation).
2. Write its key message in one sentence. If you cannot find one, that is a result too.
3. Using the four design principles and the cognitive load rules, find 3 strengths and 3 weaknesses.
4. Rework the 2 weakest slides in the "assertion + evidence" style.

## Summary

- The goal of a presentation is the audience's decision; work starts with the questions about audience, knowledge and action.
- The key message is one sentence; the pyramid principle: the conclusion first, then the arguments.
- The story: situation → complication → resolution; titles are full assertions.
- Design: contrast, repetition, alignment, proximity; reducing cognitive load.
- The chart is chosen by purpose; delivery is rehearsed and ends with a clear request.
